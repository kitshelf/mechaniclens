# Labels

The kit labels each line of the document. It does not label the shop, the price, or the repair.

## The three line labels

| Label | What it means | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | The line is a normal part of a repair estimate and its wording is clear enough to understand. | It does not mean the price is fair, the work is necessary, or the shop is honest. |
| **Check** | The line is common, but something in it is worth confirming: the amount, the quantity, the labor basis, or what it covers. | It does not mean the line is wrong. It means the document does not answer the question. |
| **Question this** | The line's wording, placement, or combination with other lines is worth a specific question before approval. | It does not mean fraud, overcharging, or bad faith. It means the line needs an answer. |

## The reading

Lead with the document type and a count. Do not produce a whole-estimate verdict.

```
Reading: Standard 12 · Check 3 · Question this 2
```

The count is the number of lines in each label. It is not a score, a percentage, or a probability. There is no "risk score," no "confidence," and no "overall verdict."

## The markers

- `[CONFIRM: ...]` — a fact the document does not state and the user must confirm. Never invent the fact.
- `[NOT SHOWN]` — a field the document leaves blank or omits. Say so; do not guess what belongs there.

## How to phrase a label

Quote the line, then name the label, then give the reason in one sentence.

> "Shop supplies — 5% of labor" — **Check**. The line is common, but the estimate does not say what the 5% covers or whether it is capped.

Do not write "this looks suspicious" or "this is probably padded." Name the pattern and the question. The user decides what it means.
