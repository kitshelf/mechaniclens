# Why MechanicLens exists

## The moment

A person stands at a repair-shop counter holding an estimate. The car is in the bay. The estimate has a diagnostic fee, a labor line, a parts line, a shop-supplies fee, an environmental fee, and a total. The person has maybe ninety seconds to approve or decline. They do not know what "book time" means, whether the diagnostic fee folds into the repair, or why there are two fee lines that both sound like disposal.

The asymmetry is not the price. It is the vocabulary.

## What already exists

The open-source world has built OBD-II dongles, photo-based damage estimators, and MCP cost-guide servers. Those tools assume hardware, a vision model, or a developer environment. They do not read the paper in the person's hand. The moment that decides the money — the paper estimate — has no decoder.

Price databases and cost guides exist, but they answer a different question ("what should this cost?"). They cannot know the person's region, parts tier, or labor rate. A made-up number is worse than no number, because it destroys the person's credibility at the counter.

## The gap

The customer needs two things the market does not provide together:

1. A plain-English reading of what each line claims to be.
2. A short, polite question script that exposes the padding without turning the conversation into a fight.

MechanicLens does both. It refuses to price the job. It labels each line Standard, Check, or Question this, quotes the line, and hands over the questions whose answers the person can verify against the estimate in hand.

## Who it is for

Car owners holding a repair estimate they are about to approve or decline. First-time car owners. Older drivers who want a script before a dealership visit. Partners decoding an estimate for each other.

## What it is not for

Accident-damage estimation (insurance photo estimators exist), DIY repair guidance (the OBD world), price arbitrage, or accusing a shop of wrongdoing. The kit decodes a document. It does not diagnose, price, or judge.
